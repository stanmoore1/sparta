# Roofline analysis — how much room is left

![roofline](roofline.png)

Generated by `make_kernels.py` (computes the kernel points) and `roofline.py`
(draws the plot) from `micro/machine_peak.out` and the callgrind trace.

## Provenance of every number

There is no PMU in this KVM guest — `perf` is unavailable and hardware counters
are not exposed — so nothing here comes from hardware performance counters. Each
axis is derived as follows, and the derivation is the honest limit of what these
numbers mean:

| quantity | how it was obtained |
|---|---|
| bandwidth roofs | **measured** by `micro/machine_peak.cpp`, sweeping working-set size |
| compute ceilings | **measured** by `micro/machine_peak.cpp`, 8 independent FMA chains |
| kernel bytes | callgrind cache simulation: (D1 read + write misses) x 64 B |
| kernel FLOPs | **counted by hand** from the source, listed in `make_kernels.py` |
| transcendental cost | **measured** by `micro/machine_peak.cpp`, in FMA-equivalents |
| kernel time | SPARTA's own per-section timers at 1M particles / 100 steps |

Two caveats that matter:

- The callgrind trace is at **100K** particles (a ~50x slowdown makes 1M
  impractical) while the timings are at **1M**. Miss counts are converted to a
  per-operation rate and applied to the 1M operation counts. Because the cell
  hash and `cells[]` arrays are 10x larger at 1M, this **understates** misses
  there. Kernel points are therefore, if anything, placed slightly too far right
  (too high an arithmetic intensity).
- Transcendentals are charged at their measured throughput cost in
  FMA-equivalents (`pow` = 36, `sqrt` = 5.7, `sincos` = 15) rather than as one
  "flop" each. Counting `pow` as a single flop would put the collide kernel
  absurdly far below the compute roof for entirely the wrong reason.

## Measured machine ceilings

`lscpu` reports a 260 MB L3 for this guest. That is the **host's** total, not
what these four vCPUs get, and using it would have drawn the roofs in the wrong
place. The measured bandwidth-vs-size curve says otherwise:

| working set | read GB/s | read+write GB/s | interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 8–32 KB | 81–98 | 155–295 | L1 |
| 64 KB–1 MB | 95–105 | 92–95 | L2 |
| 2 MB | 49 | 59 | knee |
| 4–64 MB | 23–27 | 24–27 | L3 |
| 256 MB–1 GB | 13 | 15–20 | DRAM |

So the **effective private cache is ~1–2 MB**, and anything past ~4 MB runs at
20–27 GB/s. The 1M-particle benchmark has a 96 MB particle array, squarely in
that regime.

Compute ceilings: **12.8 GFLOP/s scalar**, 51.3 AVX2, 92.9 AVX-512.
Random-gather (one 64 B line per access, dependent): **7.7 GB/s**, i.e. ~8.3 ns
per cache miss.

Transcendental throughput, and what it costs in FMA-equivalents:

| op | ns/call | FMA-equivalents |
|---|---:|---:|
| `pow(x, 0.19)` | 11.97 | 36 |
| `pow(x, 1/1.4)` | 12.03 | 36 |
| `exp2(p*log2(x))` | 9.08 | 27 |
| `sin` / `cos` | 5.00 | 15 |
| `sqrt` | 1.89 | 5.7 |

## Where the kernels land

At 1M particles, one core:

| kernel | AI (FLOP/B) | achieved GFLOP/s | achieved GB/s | % of its roof |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| move, baseline | 0.085 | 0.52 | 6.1 | 31% of DRAM roof |
| move, tuned | 0.085 | 0.65 | 7.6 | 39% of DRAM roof |
| collide, baseline | 1.00 | 0.85 | 0.8 | **6.6% of scalar compute roof** |
| collide, tuned | 1.00 | 0.95 | 1.0 | **7.4% of scalar compute roof** |
| sort, baseline | ~0 | — | 5.1 | 26% of DRAM roof |
| sort, tuned | ~0 | — | 9.3 | 47% of DRAM roof |

Underlying models, per operation:

- **move**: 12 FLOP and 141 B per particle — 3 FMAs for `x + dt*v`, three
  subtract-and-divide for the cell index, against 1.5 cache lines of streaming
  for the 96-byte particle record plus the cell lookup.
- **collide**: 249 FLOP-equivalents and 248 B per attempt — 82 for
  `test_collision` (of which 72 is the single `pow`), plus 0.75 x 223 for the
  `setup_collision` + `SCATTER_TwoBodyScattering` of an attempt that succeeds.
- **sort**: 100 B per particle per step for `sort()`, 53 B per particle for
  `reorder()`, and ~288 B per particle for the fused counting sort (three
  streaming passes). It performs no floating point at all, so it cannot be
  placed on a FLOP roofline and is reported as achieved bandwidth.

## What the plot actually tells us

**The three kernels are limited by three different things, and only one of them
has large headroom.**

1. **`sort` is bandwidth bound and now close to reasonable.** The tuned fused
   counting sort reaches 9.3 GB/s against a ~20 GB/s DRAM roof (47%). A pure
   `memcpy` of the same array measures 8.8 ns/particle and the scatter measures
   12.7 ns/particle, so the scatter is within 1.4x of a straight copy. There is
   perhaps 1.5–2x left, and it can only come from **moving fewer bytes**, not
   from better code — the work is already three streaming passes over a
   96-byte record.

2. **`move` is bandwidth bound with a latency-bound tail.** It reaches 39% of
   the DRAM roof. An isolated fast path measures 8.4 ns/particle (`micro_move`)
   against 18.5 ns/particle for the real kernel. That gap is not all one thing:
   it covers the ~1% of particles that take the slow cell-crossing path — which
   own 40% of the program's conditional and 99.8% of its indirect branch
   mispredictions — plus the `pflag` dispatch chain and counters that the
   microbenchmark omits. The two cannot be separated from these measurements,
   only bounded: if the real fast path costs the same as the isolated one, the
   slow path is of order 1 µs per particle, and if the real fast path is more
   expensive, correspondingly less. Either way the remaining headroom in `move`
   is not in the streaming arithmetic.

3. **`collide` is neither bandwidth nor compute bound — it is latency bound, and
   this is where the headroom is.** It runs at 7.4% of the scalar FMA peak and
   **4.8% of its own bandwidth roof**. Both roofs are more than an order of
   magnitude away. That gap is dependency stalls: per attempt the kernel walks
   `params[i][j]` (two dependent loads), `vremax[icell][0][0]` (three), the
   `next[]` linked list (up to ten), and then serialises on
   `vr2 -> pow -> vre -> compare`, with `pow` alone worth 36 FMAs.

The practical consequence, and it is worth stating plainly: **the kernel that
looks most "computational" has the least to do with floating point throughput.**
Collide's problem is that almost nothing in it can issue in parallel. That is
also why devirtualizing the collision math (A2) changed nothing measurable —
removing instructions from a kernel that is stalled on dependent loads does not
make it faster.

## What this predicted, and whether it held

The roofline was used to decide what to try next, and to decide what *not* to
try:

- It predicted **A5 (skip the `plist` linked-list walk when particles are already
  cell-contiguous)** would be the largest collide win, because it removes a
  ten-deep dependent load chain rather than instructions. Measured: collide
  2.75 s -> 1.98 s when contiguity holds every step. **Held.**
- It predicted **A2 (devirtualization)** would do essentially nothing, since the
  indirect branches were already perfectly predicted. Measured: no change.
  **Held.**
- It argued **B3 (splitting the 96-byte `OnePart` into hot and cold halves)**
  was not worth ~280 edit sites: it would cut `move`'s bytes by a third, but
  `sort` — the kernel most tightly bandwidth-bound — has to permute every field
  regardless, so its traffic would be unchanged, and `collide` needs `erot`/`evib`
  and would end up straddling two arrays instead of one. **Not attempted, on
  this evidence.**
- It suggested **huge pages** might help the scatter's ~100,000 open write
  destinations. Measured with `micro/micro_thp`: `MADV_HUGEPAGE` did not help
  (12.7 -> 15.3 ns/particle, i.e. slightly worse). **Refuted; dropped.**
